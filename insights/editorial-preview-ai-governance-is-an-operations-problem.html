FIELD NOTE 03 / AI GOVERNANCE

AI Governance Is an Operations Problem, Too

Responsible AI is not only about the model. It is also about ownership, decision rights, human review, escalation, evidence, monitoring, and what happens when the system behaves unexpectedly.

The model is only part of the system

Building my AI governance project reinforced something I've seen in real operational environments: a tool never operates in isolation. It sits inside a workflow with people, inputs, dependencies, decisions, handoffs, deadlines, and consequences.

For the fictional RouteAssist system, the technical use case was AI-assisted routing and operational decision support. But the governance problem was broader. Who can approve the use case? What decisions may the system influence? Which decisions must remain human? How is an override recorded? Who monitors performance? What happens when a recommendation could affect a time-sensitive medical delivery?

Human oversight has to be operational

Saying that a system has a “human in the loop” is not enough. Meaningful oversight needs authority, context, time, and a defined action. The reviewer has to know what they are responsible for, when intervention is required, how to override the recommendation, and when the issue needs escalation.

That is why I defined RouteAssist as decision support rather than an autonomous decision-maker. Consequential decisions remain with authorized personnel, and the governance boundary identifies actions the system may not perform independently.

Governance needs a lifecycle

I used the NIST AI Risk Management Framework to organize the work around Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. What stood out to me was how naturally those functions connect to operational disciplines: establish ownership, understand the environment, define measurable expectations, monitor performance, respond to problems, and improve the process.

The project therefore goes beyond an AI policy or risk register. It includes use-case boundaries, stakeholders and affected parties, risk assessment, evaluation criteria, monitoring, human review, incident response, third-party considerations, evidence, and remediation.

Exceptions and failures matter as much as normal operation

The most revealing test of a workflow is often what happens when the normal path breaks. AI governance needs that same attention. A system can perform acceptably most of the time and still create serious risk if nobody has defined how to handle drift, unexpected outputs, incomplete data, harmful impacts, security events, or material system changes.

Governance therefore needs escalation thresholds, incident ownership, stop or suspension authority, preserved evidence, and criteria for deciding when a system can return to operation.

Responsible AI has to become repeatable work

The most useful shift for me was seeing AI governance less as a set of principles and more as an operating model. Principles establish direction. The organization still needs processes that make accountability and oversight repeatable.

My focus is turning those expectations into ownership, workflows, checkpoints, evidence, escalation, and continuous improvement—so responsible AI use becomes something an organization can actually perform.
