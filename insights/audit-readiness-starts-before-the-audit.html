FIELD NOTE 05 / AUDIT READINESS + CONTROL ASSURANCE

Audit Readiness Starts Before the Audit

Audit readiness is built through everyday control execution, not last-minute evidence collection.

The Audit Shouldn't Be the Starting Point

One thing my operations experience has taught me is that preparation works best when it's part of the process, not something everyone scrambles to do before an inspection or review.

When a process is running well, you can usually see it in the work itself. Responsibilities are clear. Records are maintained. Problems are escalated. And when someone asks what happened, the team doesn't have to reconstruct the answer from memory.

I've been thinking about that same principle while developing control documentation, testing procedures, and evidence workflows for my self-directed GRC projects.

An audit might happen at a particular point in time, but the controls being evaluated operate—or should operate—long before an auditor arrives.

If evidence only becomes a priority when an audit is announced, an organization may discover that important decisions weren't documented, exceptions weren't tracked, or remediation activities never received formal closure.

At that point, collecting evidence becomes an exercise in recovering the past.

Audit readiness isn't about preparing a convincing story for an auditor. It's about operating controls consistently enough that the evidence already tells the story.

Design Evidence Into the Workflow

One of the most useful questions I've learned to ask in operations is: How will we know this actually happened?

It's a simple question, but it changes how a process is designed.

Consider an access review. The requirement might be to periodically evaluate whether users still need their assigned permissions. A procedure can explain who performs the review and how often it occurs.

But if the workflow doesn't preserve the reviewer's decision, identify exceptions, and connect requested changes to their completion, demonstrating the control's operation becomes difficult.

The same issue can appear in change management, risk assessments, policy exceptions, and other governance activities.

While building sample control and evidence workflows, I focused on connecting three things: the activity required by the control, the person responsible for performing it, and the record that demonstrates the outcome.

That doesn't mean saving every email or screenshot.

Evidence should be relevant to the control objective, sufficiently reliable, and retained in a way that allows someone else to understand what occurred. It also needs appropriate protection, because audit evidence may contain sensitive business or access information.

A well-designed workflow produces useful records as a natural result of doing the work.

The strongest evidence process isn't the one that collects the most files. It's the one that consistently captures what matters while the work is happening.

Test the Control Before Someone Else Does

A documented control and an operating control are not necessarily the same thing.

A policy might require timely access removal when an employee changes roles. A procedure might identify the manager and system administrator responsible for the handoff.

Those documents establish expectations. They don't, by themselves, demonstrate that the expectations are being met.

That's where control testing becomes valuable.

In my self-directed GRC work, I've approached testing as a way to connect a control's stated purpose with observable evidence of its operation.

For example, a test of role-change access removal might examine a defined sample of personnel changes, identify the access modifications required, compare completion dates against the organization's criteria, and inspect supporting records.

The test should be specific enough that another reviewer could understand what was examined, which criteria were applied, and how the conclusion was reached.

It's also important to distinguish between a control that's poorly designed and one that's well designed but inconsistently executed.

Those problems call for different responses.

A missing approval step may indicate a design weakness. A required approval that exists in the procedure but is repeatedly skipped may indicate an operating effectiveness problem.

Both matter, but understanding the difference makes remediation more useful.

Testing isn't just about finding failures. It's about understanding whether a control is designed to achieve its objective and whether it operates as intended.

Make Remediation Part of the Process

Finding a control gap is an important outcome of testing. Leaving it unresolved is a different story.

I've seen how easily unresolved issues can linger when nobody owns the follow-up. The same risk exists in GRC.

Suppose a sample review identifies access removals that occurred later than the organization's established timeframe.

The finding should lead to more than a note that the control failed.

Someone needs to determine why the delays occurred. Was the handoff unclear? Were requests submitted late? Was there insufficient visibility into outstanding work? Did the procedure create an unrealistic dependency?

The answers help determine whether the response should involve a workflow change, clearer ownership, additional monitoring, or another corrective action.

A useful remediation record should identify the issue, its risk or impact, the accountable owner, the corrective action, and the expected completion date.

Just as importantly, closure should depend on evidence that the action was implemented and, where appropriate, that it addressed the underlying weakness.

Not every finding can be resolved immediately. When remediation takes time, interim measures and risk acceptance decisions may also need to be documented and approved.

While developing sample issue-tracking and control-testing artifacts, I've come to see remediation as part of the control lifecycle rather than a separate administrative exercise.

A finding becomes valuable when it leads to an owned action, a verified correction, and a more reliable process.

Build Readiness Into Normal Operations

An organization shouldn't need an entirely different way of working during audit season.

There will always be additional coordination when an audit begins. Evidence requests need to be managed, questions need answers, and testing may require access to specific records or personnel.

But that coordination should build on an existing foundation.

Control owners should understand their responsibilities. Evidence should be retained according to established requirements. Exceptions should have visible resolution paths. Testing results should be traceable. And unresolved issues should already be known to the people responsible for managing them.

That kind of readiness depends on operational habits, not just documentation.

It also depends on making the process practical for the people doing the work.

If evidence requirements are confusing, ownership is fragmented, or every review depends on someone manually chasing updates, consistency becomes difficult to sustain.

The intersection that interests me most is where a governance requirement meets the realities of time, staffing, handoffs, and competing priorities.

The objective isn't to create more paperwork.

It's to make accountability, documentation, testing, and improvement part of the way work gets done.

Audit readiness becomes sustainable when the process supports it every day—not only when someone asks to see the evidence.

The Lesson I'm Taking Forward

Developing these control-testing and evidence workflows has changed how I think about audits.

I started by focusing on what an auditor would need to review: control descriptions, supporting records, testing results, and remediation documentation.

But the deeper lesson was about the systems that produce those records.

When responsibilities are clear, controls are executed consistently, and issues are addressed as they arise, audit preparation becomes less about assembling a defense and more about demonstrating the work.

That's a connection I recognize from operations.

Reliable outcomes rarely come from last-minute effort alone. They come from repeatable processes, clear ownership, and the discipline to follow through when something doesn't work.

That distinction now informs how I approach GRC: audit readiness is an outcome of effective governance, not a substitute for it.

The best time to prepare for an audit isn't when the request arrives. It's when the control is being designed, performed, tested, and improved.
