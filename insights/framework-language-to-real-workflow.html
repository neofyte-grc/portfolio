FIELD NOTE 02 / GRC ENGINEERING

From Framework Language to Real Workflow

A control statement tells you what needs to be true. Operations has to answer who does it, when it happens, what happens next, and how anyone can prove it worked.

The gap between requirement and execution

One of the most useful lessons from building my framework-to-workflow project was how much work exists between a governance requirement and a functioning control. A framework may establish an outcome such as limiting access, reviewing privileges, or removing access when it is no longer needed. That direction matters, but it does not run the process.

Someone still has to translate the requirement into roles, triggers, approvals, actions, evidence, deadlines, exceptions, and escalation paths. That translation is where my operations background feels especially relevant.

Start with the operating question

For an access-control requirement, I found it useful to ask practical questions first. What starts the workflow? Who requests the access? Who has enough context to approve it? Who provisions it? Who verifies the change? Where is the evidence retained? What happens when the request is urgent, unusual, or incomplete?

Those questions turn abstract control language into an operating model. In the project, that became seven connected IAM workflows covering joiners, movers, leavers, access requests, provisioning, periodic reviews, privileged access, emergency revocation, and exceptions.

Traceability changes the quality of the design

I also learned that a workflow becomes much easier to defend when each step can be traced backward and forward. I used a chain that connects business requirement, risk, framework requirement, control objective, workflow, owner, evidence, test, metric, exception, and improvement.

That structure forces useful questions. If a step has no owner, who is accountable? If a control has no evidence, how will it be assessed? If a metric misses its threshold, who responds? If an exception never expires, is it still an exception or has it quietly become the normal process?

Evidence should be designed in, not added later

A process can operate correctly and still be difficult to assess if nobody decided what evidence should exist. Building sample request records, review records, control-test workpapers, and remediation records reinforced the value of making evidence part of the workflow itself.

The goal is not documentation for its own sake. The goal is to create enough reliable information to show what decision was made, by whom, under what authority, and whether the expected control activity actually occurred.

Frameworks become valuable through execution

I do not see frameworks as checklists to copy into a policy. I see them as structured sources of security outcomes that still require operational design. The work is in converting those outcomes into processes that people can execute consistently and that an organization can monitor, test, and improve.

That is the bridge I want my GRC work to demonstrate: not just knowing the language of a framework, but being able to carry its intent into the way work gets done.
