FIELD NOTE 01 / OPERATIONS + SECURITY

Security Has to Survive Contact With Operations

A security control can be technically correct and still fail in practice. The difference often comes down to whether the control was designed for the environment—and the people—expected to carry it out.

My background in operations and logistics taught me to pay attention to the gap between how a process is supposed to work and how work actually gets done. On paper, a procedure can look complete. In practice, people are balancing deadlines, handoffs, competing priorities, incomplete information, and the pressure to keep the operation moving.

That experience shapes how I think about security today. Governance, risk, and compliance are not only documentation exercises. A policy, control, or workflow has to function inside a real operating environment. If it cannot, the organization may have a control on paper without having the behavior or evidence the control was intended to produce.

The control is only the beginning

A framework can tell us what outcome is required: restrict access, review risk, retain evidence, approve changes, train users, or escalate exceptions. The harder question is operational: who performs the action, when does it happen, what information do they need, where is the decision recorded, and what happens when the normal process breaks?

Those questions are familiar to anyone who has worked in operations. They are questions of ownership, sequencing, handoffs, exception management, and accountability. They are also security questions.

A control that depends on people doing the right thing needs a process that makes the right thing clear, practical, and repeatable.

Design for the real workflow

When a security requirement adds friction without accounting for the surrounding workflow, people will naturally look for a faster path. That does not automatically mean the workforce is the problem. It can mean the process was designed without enough understanding of the work.

For me, human-centered security starts by understanding that operating context. Where does the control enter the workflow? Who owns it? Does that person have the authority and information to act? Is the expected behavior clear? Can completion be evidenced without creating unnecessary administrative work? What is the escalation path when the standard process does not fit?

This is where operations experience becomes useful in GRC. The objective is not to weaken a requirement to make it convenient. The objective is to translate the requirement into a process that preserves the security intent while giving people a realistic way to execute it.

Make evidence part of the process

A repeatable workflow should also make evidence easier to produce. If a team performs a review but the result is scattered across messages, spreadsheets, and individual memory, demonstrating that the control operated consistently becomes difficult.

Good operational design connects the action, the owner, the decision, and the evidence. That creates a stronger control environment and makes later review less dependent on reconstructing what happened.

Security is an operating discipline

This is the perspective I am carrying into the projects in this portfolio. Whether I am working through a GRC control, an AI governance decision, or a cloud security process, I keep returning to the same question: what does this requirement look like when real people have to perform it repeatedly?

Security becomes more durable when controls are not treated as instructions that sit outside the operation, but as part of how the operation works. The framework defines the destination. The workflow is how the organization gets there.

This field note is part of an ongoing series documenting what I am learning while translating operations experience into practical governance, risk, compliance, cloud security, and AI governance work.
